Walking Japan: Audio Tours

An offline-first audio tour app for independent travelers—conceived, designed, built, and launched from scratch.

The problem

Travelers need flexible, high-quality tours without group schedules, roaming costs, or a constant connection.

The pivotal decision

Narrow a global idea to Tokyo, prioritize offline behavior, and rebuild the audio architecture around persistent state and streams.

What it proved

I can move from customer insight to a shipped product while learning Flutter, Dart, state management, mapping, and audio systems along the way.

Read the build story+

The idea came from a gap I felt while traveling. Audio tours made European cities more rewarding, but that same quality and flexibility disappeared in destinations outside their coverage. Rather than begin with “build a global platform,” I reduced the idea to one useful test: could I create a genuinely good offline walking-tour experience for Tokyo?

The hardest part was not the interface—it was playback. Audio had to continue across screens, remember position, survive interruptions, and work without a connection. Early implementations became tangled as different components fought over state. I stepped back, mapped the behaviors, moved toward persistent app state and stream-based updates, and rebuilt the player around how a traveler actually moves through a tour.

The project taught me to separate the product promise from the implementation I happened to start with. FlutterFlow accelerated the visible experience, but custom Flutter and Dart work was essential for the differentiating features. The result shipped to Google Play with downloadable tours, offline maps, GPS context, and continuous audio—proof that I could carry a product from personal insight through architecture, iteration, and launch.

Podcast A-to-Z

A document-to-podcast workflow that turned an internal request into a practical lesson in resilient cloud architecture.

The first constraint

A synchronous prototype hit payload limits and timeouts as soon as documents and processing became substantial.

The rethink

Re-architect uploads and generation as an asynchronous workflow using S3, Lambda, and Step Functions.

What it proved

Good product building means learning from system limits, modularizing failure points, and changing the design—not defending the first idea.

Read the build story+

This project started with a conversation, not a feature list. A colleague wanted one specific capability from a NotebookLM-like experience: turn source material into a useful podcast. Because I had already worked with text-to-speech and programmatic audio editing, I treated the request as a chance to build something practical while deepening my AWS knowledge.

The first version put everything into one synchronous path. It worked for tiny inputs, then broke against real constraints: browser payload limits, processing time, and request timeouts. That failure made the architecture problem visible. I moved uploads to S3 and broke summarization and podcast generation into an asynchronous, modular workflow using Lambda and Step Functions, allowing the front end to track progress instead of waiting for one fragile request.

The most valuable outcome was the shift in my thinking. AI could produce code quickly, but it did not remove the need to understand constraints, permissions, failure modes, and downstream dependencies. I learned to use prototypes to expose system limits, then redesign around those limits rather than endlessly patching the original implementation.

AI Launch Operations

A portfolio of focused AI tools that removes repetitive work from launch management and gives teams more time for judgment.

The opportunity

Launch teams were spending too much time finding inputs, formatting updates, and rebuilding standard documents.

The approach

Build agents around real templates, reference material, and existing workflows rather than asking teams to adapt to generic AI.

The impact

More than 80% less time producing key launch documents, with faster reporting and more consistent outputs.

Read the build story+

I saw a recurring pattern in launch operations: skilled people were spending disproportionate time collecting inputs, recreating standard documents, and formatting status updates. The opportunity was not simply to “add AI.” It was to redesign those workflows so AI could handle the repetitive assembly while people retained ownership of accuracy, judgment, and cross-functional decisions.

I built focused tools around the organization's existing templates and source material, including launch-document generators, reporting workflows, transcription utilities, and an analysis assistant for customer feedback. That grounding mattered. Instead of asking teams to learn generic prompting, the tools met them inside familiar processes and produced outputs shaped around real operating standards.

The document workflows reduced creation time by more than 80%, and reporting shifted from manual reconstruction toward review and decision-making. The broader lesson was that adoption follows usefulness: start with a painful recurring job, preserve human accountability, prove value in a working team, and only then scale the mechanism.
